_Stories from the Road The Village Baker_

## The Village Baker

_By Tanner Cranor_

Deep in the mountains, where the water runs clear and the breeze is bridled by the weathered peaks, there lived a town full of hard-working people and one hard-working baker.

The baker was a proud man and always made sure that his bakery windows were filled with glistening pastries and towering cakes. He loved to see the awe of the children as they passed by with their parents on errands. He dreamed of the day when people would come from across the sea to visit his fine bakery. He worked hard towards his dream. After he’d saved for some time, he built the most magnificent oven there ever was, complete with contraptions that would assemble cakes right as they came out of their pans. If everything went just right, it could assemble a cake taller than a man after one bake. It was a true delight to the townspeople and baked more cake than they could eat at just one party.

One night, after one such party had ended, the wind blew east along the mountains, and a great storm rolled in over the peaks like no one there had ever seen.

No one in town could sleep through the destruction that ensued. Trees fell. Animals panicked and burst from their pens. Roofs blew apart and shingles scattered across the town. Carts could be seen spinning and scooting along the roads as if tied to wild unseen mustangs. The mountain itself seemed to shake and tremble with each clap of thunder and bolt of lightning.

When the storm passed, the townspeople crept out of their homes and shops to survey the damage.

The baker hardly dared to move. He had seen the storm from his window, felt the angry gusts of wind shake the bakery walls, and worst of all he had heard the terrible splintering squeal of an old dying tree as it twisted and split and came crashing through the roof of the bakery. The only thing he could hope for was that his new oven might still be intact.

Slowly he crept down the stairs from his room to see the damage. The anxious pressure in his chest dropped straight into his stomach as he found that his worst fears had come true. The old oak had ripped right through his kitchen as it had fallen. The oven that was on the way to making him world-famous had been obliterated.

He scooped up crumbling bricks and his heart seemed to crumble too. “I cannot fix this,” he cried, “and I have no money to rebuild.” He looked outside to the busy town, “I must ask for help!”

But there was no help to be found. “We are sorry,” replied the town’s chief councilman, “but there is no money to loan, nor help to be had. We have to clean out the wells and repair the roads and the light posts and the school for the children.”

“The children!” the baker shouted in frustration. His sorrow turning to anger in an instant. “What will the children do at school if they have no bread to eat! There will be nothing baked for the entire town if my bakery cannot be fixed!”

The village councilmen looked worried, but instead of shouting, one of the councilmen laid his hand on the baker’s shoulder and handed him a key. “We don’t want that.” He said in a quiet voice, “and we know that you don’t either. I have work to do here, and my home will be empty tonight. Go and rest until the morning and we will see what we can muster to fix your bakery tomorrow.” The baker was still furious and so could think of nothing to say to such a kind response. He accepted the key and huffed away towards the councilman’s quarters.

As he stomped up a hill, he passed family after family, each hard at work to restore order to their homes. His stomp soon turned into a stiff stride, then a slow walk, until finally he could go no further. He paused his ascent and sank into the shadow of the nearby homes to watch the scene around him. He watched a little boy and girl herding frightened goats back under a hastily reconstructed shelter while their mother swept at drifted piles of broken shingles. He turned and looked back down the road to see other families performing similar chores, but what stood out to him most were the children. None of them complained as they worked. These were tasks many would have begged to avoid, especially given the hour of night, but under these circumstances he heard not even a whimper or sigh.

There was no way the baker could go to sleep with such brave little volunteers bustling around him. How could he complain when they worked so quietly and faithfully? As his hard heart began to melt, he felt sorry for the way he’d shouted at the councilmembers. The key weighed especially heavy in his hand and so he stuffed it into his pocket and turned back towards the center of town.

This time when he arrived at his bakery, he waded through the wreckage and stepped over his fallen oven to grab his apron. He dusted it off and turned to his cupboards. Flour had settled over the rubble like snow, but most of his ingredients were fine. He began to mix dough for bread, all the while thinking of those brave little children. He was thinking of the two siblings herding goats when he noticed an old crockery pot peeking out of a damaged cupboard, and he got an idea.

Leaving his dough to rise, he collected bits of broken brick to make a firepit in the yard. There he started a fire. He mixed oats and shavings of his finest chocolate into a cookie dough and filled the pot with a mound of the sticky mixture. It was no shining pastry but, when he pulled the pot off the fire a little while later and cracked open the lid, the smell was mesmerizing.

Morning came the next day and the children in town were the first to rise. No one quite knew the smell in the air, but it was irresistible. Parents’ tired eyes brightened, and the children got clean and dressed quickly that day. There was still work to be done, but before long everyone had gathered at the village center. In the night, the city council had arrived early to help the baker heave and shove the massive oak tree out of the rubble and into the yard. It now lay across the front length of the bakery and its broad trunk was littered with baskets of bread and bricks of cookie wrapped in paper. There the baker allowed each family to take a loaf of bread and a bundle of gooey cookies for their children.

In not-so-many days, the town was set right again. The roads and lamps were fixed. Wells were cleared and clean again. The school was repaired. Even the baker’s oven was rebuilt and the bakery was reconstructed bigger and better than ever before. Now, people do come from far and wide to see the bakery, but many, many more come to see the baker. For whenever he sees a child at his door, he lays down his work, brushes off his apron, and visits with the child and their parents. He tells them the story of his cookies and, if they have tended their chores that day without complaint, he sends them to pick a lumpy brick of cookie from the oak table by the door.
